# 1 Introduction

The familiar distinction between nature and nurture offers an intuition for how computational systems are shaped. Some properties are specified in advance and provide a fixed framework, whereas others are acquired from experience and remain more adaptable. Algorithms and neural networks differ in a similar, though not absolute, fashion. An algorithm's objective and procedure are explicitly prescribed. A neural network also operates within a designed architecture and objective, but its internal parameters are learned from data. It can therefore capture and potentially reuse statistical regularities that were not individually formulated as rules. This adaptability comes with weaker guarantees because what the network learns may no longer remain reliable outside its training distribution. [Veličković and Blundell, 2021]

At first glance, algorithms and neural networks therefore seem to sit at opposing ends of a spectrum. Algorithms excel at concrete tasks such as sorting lists, traversing graphs, performing arithmetic and comparing sequences because a set of instructions determines how their input will be processed. For a correctly implemented algorithm and inputs satisfying its assumptions, its result is reproducible and its computational complexity can be analysed in advance. Neural networks, by contrast, learn approximate input--output mappings from examples. They can compress objects into representations that may remain useful across related inputs or tasks, but their behaviour is established empirically rather than guaranteed over every valid input. In particular, a network that performs reliably on its training distribution may still fail when the properties of its inputs change. [Veličković and Blundell, 2021; Xhonneux et al., 2021]

Understanding these complementary strengths and limitations motivates why one might push a neural network to accomplish something that an algorithm can already do. Can the generalisation potential of a learned representation be combined with the specificity of an algorithmically defined target? This ambition is studied more broadly under neural algorithmic reasoning, where neural networks are trained to reproduce or execute algorithmic computations with the aim of acquiring more systematic behaviour. [Veličković and Blundell, 2021] The present work approaches this ambition through a narrower question. It does not attempt to extract a runnable algorithm from a neural network or reproduce the internal execution steps of an existing procedure. Instead, it investigates whether a neural network can learn a useful approximation of an algorithm's output relationship and how far that approximation survives outside its training distribution.

Sequence comparison provides a controlled setting in which to investigate this tension. Sequences occur throughout computer science and bioinformatics, where they may represent text, DNA, RNA, proteins or derived structural descriptions. One of the most established measures of sequence dissimilarity is the Levenshtein edit distance. It is defined as the minimum number of insertions, deletions and substitutions required to transform one sequence into another. A dynamic-programming algorithm computes this value exactly for arbitrary symbol strings, with a worst-case time complexity of \(O(nm)\) for sequences of lengths \(n\) and \(m\). A smaller edit distance indicates that fewer changes separate the strings and therefore corresponds to greater global similarity. [Levenshtein, 1966; Berger, Waterman and Yu, 2021]

Levenshtein distance is particularly suitable for the present investigation because its value does not depend on the origin or meaning of the symbols. The same operation can be applied to synthetic strings, natural amino-acid sequences, secondary-structure strings or 3Di representations. Normalising the distance by sequence length additionally produces a common similarity scale for strings of different sizes. The resulting global normalised Levenshtein similarity therefore provides a consistent and exactly computable target across differing sequence distributions. This makes it possible to ask whether a neural network preserves transferable properties of the target function or merely captures regularities of the strings on which it was trained.

This global target must be distinguished from local sequence similarity. In bioinformatics, tools such as BLASTp search for strong local alignments: two proteins may share a similar region or domain even when their complete sequences differ substantially. Global normalised Levenshtein similarity instead evaluates the transformations required across the complete strings. The two measures are related through their common concern with sequence resemblance, but they answer different questions. Existing tools such as BLAST and MMseqs2 already support large-scale biological sequence search; the purpose of this thesis is therefore not to present neural embeddings as a solution to an otherwise unsolved search problem. Rather, global Levenshtein similarity is used as a controlled algorithmic ground truth against which the geometry and transfer behaviour of learned representations can be measured. [Altschul et al., 1990; Steinegger and Söding, 2017]

An embedding maps an object to a fixed-dimensional vector. If the embedding preserves a chosen relationship, objects that are similar under that relationship should also be positioned close to one another in the resulting vector space. Embeddings have become important representations for text, images and biological sequences. BERT learns contextual representations of language, CLIP relates images and text in a shared representation space, and protein language models such as ProtTrans and ESM learn representations from large collections of amino-acid sequences. [Devlin et al., 2019; Radford et al., 2021; Elnaggar et al., 2022; Lin et al., 2023] These models are not trained specifically to reproduce global normalised Levenshtein similarity. Nevertheless, their geometry may contain sequence relationships that emerge from their broader self-supervised training objectives.

Evidence that such a signal already exists was provided by a comparison between protein-language-model embeddings and BLASTp similarity. On a CAFA3 subset, cosine similarities derived from ESM-1b representations followed an interval-averaged local BLASTp similarity curve with a Spearman rank correlation of approximately \(\rho=0.66\). [Fenoy, Edera and Stegmayer, 2022] This result shows that a general, task-agnostic protein embedding is not blind to sequence similarity even though it was not trained explicitly for that comparison. However, it does not establish that ESM preserves global normalised Levenshtein similarity. BLASTp measures local alignment relationships, the reported correlation was calculated after aggregating observations into similarity intervals, and the evaluated protein pool differs from the data used in this thesis. The value \(\rho=0.66\) is therefore not a performance level that ESM-2 should be expected to reproduce here. It licenses the more limited but important premise that the geometry of a general-purpose protein embedding can already contain a measurable local sequence-similarity signal.

This observation creates the opening addressed by the present work. If local similarity is already reflected in a task-agnostic representation, does the same representation also carry a global similarity signal? If it does, can a small network trained specifically on global normalised Levenshtein similarity sharpen that signal? More importantly, if the task-specific network is trained on one distribution and evaluated on another, which parts of its learned approximation remain useful? This last question distinguishes performance on familiar data from abstraction beyond the training distribution.

Previous work has already established that edit distance can be approximated through learned embeddings. CNN-ED maps strings into a fixed-dimensional Euclidean space and trains the vector distances to approximate edit distance, demonstrating that convolutional encoders can support edit-distance-based similarity search. [Dai et al., 2020] NeuroSEED develops the same general principle for biological sequences and investigates how different encoders and embedding geometries preserve sequence distances. [Corso et al., 2021] These studies establish the embedding principle on which this thesis builds. They also motivate two central design choices: a convolutional sequence encoder and a shared metric space in which the distance between two encoded sequences is supervised directly by a sequence-distance target. Their principal evaluations, however, do not establish how the same frozen encoder behaves across the synthetic-to-natural and representation shifts examined here. Chapter 2 discusses these relationships in detail.

Based on these ideas, this work develops SNNEED, a small task-specific Siamese encoder for global normalised Levenshtein similarity. The same encoder processes both members of a sequence pair and maps each sequence to a 128-dimensional unit vector. Their Euclidean distance is converted directly into a predicted similarity, without a separate prediction head. SNNEED is trained on synthetically generated sequence pairs using exact global normalised Levenshtein similarity as supervision. After training, its parameters are frozen and the same encoder is evaluated on independent synthetic strings, natural amino-acid sequences, and protein-derived 3Di and secondary-structure representations. These data differ in their similarity-score distributions, character frequencies and order-dependent sequence patterns. The protein-derived representations are used as symbolic evaluation distributions; no claim is made that unit-cost Levenshtein similarity predicts protein homology, structure or function.

The frozen representation of ESM-2 provides a task-agnostic point of comparison. On amino-acid sequences, it tests whether global Levenshtein information is already present in a general protein representation before task-specific training. On 3Di and secondary-structure strings, both ESM-2 and SNNEED receive symbols through vocabularies originally associated with amino acids. Their outputs in these settings are therefore not statements about biological understanding. Instead, they serve as controls for whether any useful string-similarity geometry survives when the character statistics and representation change. A non-learned trigram Dice similarity supplies an additional lexical reference.

The investigation is guided by two research questions:

> **Q1 -- Signal and task-specific improvement:** A general, task-agnostic protein embedding already carries a local similarity signal. Does it also carry a global similarity signal, and can the task-specific SNNEED improve this signal?

> **Q2 -- Abstraction and transfer:** Can SNNEED abstract beyond its training distribution? When trained on one data distribution and tested on another, did it learn transferable properties of the operation or merely regularities of the training data?

The second question is intentionally posed as a tension rather than a binary conclusion. Transfer without retraining would provide evidence that the approximation is not restricted to the exact training distribution, but it would not prove that SNNEED recovered the dynamic-programming procedure. The network may instead use transferable cues such as sequence length, composition or local patterns to approximate the algorithm's output. Consequently, this work operationalises abstraction as the degree and regime in which similarity fidelity survives distribution shift, rather than as a claim that the algorithm itself has been learned.

To answer these questions, task-agnostic and task-specific representations are compared through complementary views of the predicted similarity geometry. Rank correlation measures whether the overall ordering of sequence similarities is retained; high-similarity discrimination tests whether close pairs can be separated from the remaining pool; and retrieval evaluation tests whether highly similar partners can be recovered for a query. The trained model is then held fixed while the input distribution moves from synthetic training strings to natural amino-acid sequences and to 3Di and secondary-structure representations. Detailed definitions of the representations, targets and evaluation measures are provided in Chapter 3, while Chapter 4 specifies the model and experimental protocol.

The remainder of this thesis is organised as follows. Chapter 2 reviews neural algorithmic reasoning, similarity signals in protein embeddings, and learned edit-distance embeddings, with particular attention to CNN-ED and NeuroSEED. Chapter 3 introduces the algorithmic, representational and evaluation foundations required to understand the study. Chapter 4 describes the design and implementation of SNNEED, the training data and the experimental protocol. Chapter 5 presents the results objectively around the two research questions. Chapter 6 interprets these results, examines the limits of the abstraction claim and distinguishes algorithmic approximation from biological similarity. Chapter 7 summarises the conclusions and outlines directions for further work.

# 2 Related Work

## 2.1 Neural algorithmic reasoning

Neural algorithmic reasoning investigates how neural networks can be combined with algorithmic computation. Classical algorithms apply a prescribed sequence of operations to inputs satisfying a defined set of conditions. Neural networks instead infer their internal parameters from examples and are consequently more adaptable but less amenable to universal guarantees. Neural algorithmic reasoning seeks to combine these properties by training neural models to reproduce algorithmic computations, sometimes supervising both their final outputs and their intermediate execution states. [Veličković and Blundell, 2021]

This programme provides the conceptual horizon for the present work, but its strict definition is broader and stronger than the claim made here. SNNEED is trained from sequence pairs and final normalised Levenshtein targets; it is not shown the dynamic-programming table, edit operations or intermediate states of the Levenshtein algorithm. It therefore learns an approximation of the input--output function rather than an explicitly aligned execution procedure. This distinction prevents successful prediction or transfer from being interpreted as proof that the algorithm itself has been recovered.

Generalisation remains a central challenge in both settings. A learned reasoner may reproduce an algorithm on inputs resembling its training instances without acquiring the systematic behaviour that the corresponding classical procedure provides across its complete domain. Studies of transfer between learned graph algorithms show that standard fine-tuning or parameter freezing does not automatically produce systematic transfer and that additional capacity alone does not substitute for an appropriate inductive bias. [Xhonneux et al., 2021] Although those models, algorithms and forms of supervision differ from SNNEED, the conclusion motivates an explicit measurement of what survives when a learned approximation leaves its training distribution.

## 2.2 Similarity signals in general-purpose protein embeddings

Protein language models learn representations through self-supervised objectives applied to large collections of protein sequences. Their learned spaces can subsequently support tasks for which they were not trained explicitly. A broad comparison of protein representation methods evaluated whether their embedding geometries reflect sequence similarity, protein domains and functional annotations. [Fenoy, Edera and Stegmayer, 2022] For sequence similarity, representation-space cosine similarity was compared with BLASTp, providing evidence that general-purpose embeddings contain a measurable local similarity signal.

This result is directly relevant because it provides a task-agnostic starting point. It suggests that learning from large protein corpora can organise sequences in a way that partially reflects their resemblance without direct supervision from an edit-distance objective. At the same time, it leaves two gaps. First, BLASTp describes local alignment similarity and therefore cannot establish whether the embeddings preserve a global relationship over complete sequences. Second, observing such a signal in a pretrained model does not show how much it can be improved through task-specific learning. The first research question of this thesis addresses both gaps by comparing frozen ESM-2 geometry with a network supervised directly by global normalised Levenshtein similarity.

The comparison must remain qualified. ESM-2 is not a peer edit-distance approximator and was not trained for the target used here. Its frozen embedding is included to determine whether an incidental global signal exists and whether task-specific supervision changes the resulting geometry. Its outputs on secondary-structure and 3Di strings are additionally treated as controls for representation shift, not as measurements of ESM-2's biological capabilities on those representations.

## 2.3 CNN-ED

CNN-ED directly establishes that edit distance can be represented through a learned Euclidean embedding. It maps a string to a fixed-dimensional vector using a convolutional neural network and trains vector distances to approximate the corresponding edit distances. Its objective combines an approximation loss, which encourages numerical agreement with edit distance, with a triplet loss, which encourages the correct ordering of nearby and distant strings. [Dai et al., 2020] The method therefore addresses two properties that are also important in this thesis: value fidelity and the preservation of neighbour order.

The use of convolution is not merely incidental to CNN-ED. Convolutional filters provide a translation-equivariant way of detecting local character patterns, while pooling converts variable-length sequences into fixed-length representations. CNN-ED argues that these operations are suitable for preserving basic edit relationships and demonstrates its method on several forms of string data, including a UniRef protein dataset. It therefore precedes the present work both as a learned edit-distance embedding and as an application of that principle to protein strings. [Dai et al., 2020]

SNNEED inherits the general idea of processing strings convolutionally and embedding them in a fixed-dimensional Euclidean space. It does not reproduce CNN-ED's architecture or combined objective directly. SNNEED uses a smaller encoder, adaptive pooling and a direct parameter-free conversion from the distance between unit-normalised embeddings to predicted global normalised Levenshtein similarity. These choices are specified in Chapter 4.

The difference most relevant to the research questions lies in the evaluation objective. CNN-ED is designed and evaluated as a string-similarity-search method, with models fitted to particular datasets and assessed on held-out strings from the corresponding setting. This demonstrates within-setting edit-distance approximation, but does not establish how the same trained encoder behaves after substantial shifts in score distribution, character frequencies and order patterns. The present work makes that transfer behaviour an explicit experimental object: one SNNEED encoder is trained on synthetic strings and then applied without retraining to natural amino-acid, 3Di and secondary-structure distributions.

## 2.4 NeuroSEED

NeuroSEED extends learned distance embeddings to biological sequences and studies how sequence distances can be represented in different geometric spaces. A shared encoder maps each sequence to an embedding, and distances between embeddings are trained to approximate sequence-derived target distances. The framework considers multiple neural encoders and compares Euclidean with alternative geometries, including hyperbolic spaces designed to represent hierarchical relationships. [Corso et al., 2021] It then applies these embeddings to edit-distance approximation and downstream bioinformatics tasks such as clustering and multiple-sequence alignment.

NeuroSEED contributes two ideas that are central to SNNEED. First, it treats the distance between two encoded sequences as the primary prediction rather than relying on an unconstrained pairwise classification head. This encourages the representation space itself to preserve the supervised relationship. Second, it demonstrates that the choice of geometry is part of the model: the target is not only predicted numerically but encoded into the organisation of the latent space. SNNEED adopts this shared-encoder metric-learning principle but restricts its representation to a 128-dimensional Euclidean unit sphere and its target to global unit-cost normalised Levenshtein similarity.

The biological scope of NeuroSEED and this thesis is nevertheless different. NeuroSEED uses distance embeddings to support biological analyses and investigates geometries that capture evolutionary and hierarchical structure. The present work does not seek to model evolutionary distance or infer biological relationships. Its protein-derived datasets provide distinct symbolic distributions on which the transfer of a globally defined string-similarity approximation can be tested. NeuroSEED therefore supplies an important methodological precedent without licensing a biological interpretation of SNNEED's predictions.

As with CNN-ED, NeuroSEED evaluates learned models on multiple datasets and tasks, but this does not by itself establish the particular train-once transfer tested here. The claim made in this thesis is therefore not that previous methods never considered generalisation. It is narrower: their reported results do not answer how a single encoder trained only on the synthetic distribution used here behaves when frozen and applied to the natural AA, 3Di and secondary-structure distributions used here.

## 2.5 Exact neural execution as a boundary case

A separate line of work asks whether the Levenshtein algorithm can be implemented exactly by a neural network. A constructed ReLU network can reproduce the dynamic-programming recurrence through modules implementing character matching and minimum operations. [Ohtomo, Takasu and Akutsu, 2025] This establishes that exact neural realisation is possible under a carefully designed construction. It does not show that the same network can be discovered through ordinary gradient-based learning; the reported training experiments fail to recover the exact construction reliably.

This boundary case sharpens the scope of SNNEED. The aim is not exact algorithm realisation, extraction of pseudocode or reproduction of the dynamic-programming execution trace. SNNEED learns a lower-dimensional metric representation whose distances approximate the final global normalised Levenshtein relationship. Approximation is therefore a deliberate property of the problem formulation rather than an unsuccessful attempt to claim exact neural execution.

## 2.6 Positioning and design lineage of SNNEED

The preceding work contributes different parts of the problem addressed by SNNEED. Neural algorithmic reasoning supplies the broader motivation to combine algorithmically specified structure with learned representations, while also exposing systematic generalisation as a central difficulty. The protein-embedding study demonstrates that task-agnostic representation geometry can already contain a local sequence-similarity signal. CNN-ED establishes convolutional Euclidean embeddings as a practical approach to edit-distance approximation. NeuroSEED develops the shared-encoder distance-learning formulation for biological sequences and emphasises that the embedding geometry itself should carry the target relationship. The exact ReLU construction clarifies the boundary between approximating an algorithm's output and executing the procedure itself.

SNNEED combines these ideas in a deliberately small model. Both input strings are processed by the same convolutional encoder, variable-length representations are converted to a fixed size through adaptive pooling, and the final projection produces 128-dimensional unit vectors. The Euclidean chord distance between the two vectors is converted directly to a similarity prediction and trained against exact global normalised Levenshtein similarity with an unweighted mean-squared error. No learned prediction head sits on top of the pair. Consequently, the supervised relationship must be expressed in the embedding geometry that is later used for evaluation and retrieval.

The contribution is not the invention of edit-distance embeddings, convolutional sequence encoders or Siamese metric learning. It lies in using these established components to address the two specific questions formulated in Chapter 1. First, frozen ESM-2 and SNNEED are used to separate an incidental global similarity signal from one sharpened through direct task-specific supervision. Second, the same trained SNNEED encoder is evaluated without retraining across a ladder of distribution shifts. That experiment measures how much of the learned similarity geometry survives beyond the synthetic training distribution and where it ceases to be reliable.

This positioning also fixes the interpretation of the final results. If SNNEED preserves global similarity and retrieves close neighbours, it has learned a useful approximation of the target relationship. If part of that performance survives on natural AA, 3Di and secondary-structure strings, the approximation contains features that transfer beyond the training distribution. Neither observation alone proves that dynamic programming, or the Levenshtein operation as a symbolic procedure, is encoded in the network. The research questions are therefore answered through measured fidelity and transfer, while the stronger claim of algorithm learning remains outside the scope of the thesis.
