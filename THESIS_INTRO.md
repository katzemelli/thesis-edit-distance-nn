Can a neural network learn an algorithm? The orthodox answer is a qualified no. Under the standard reading of the universal-approximation results, a network performs statistical function approximation rather than symbolic computation (**"Multilayer feedforward networks are universal approximators", Hornik, Stinchcombe & White, 1989**): it fits a smooth map to the examples it is shown, and there is no procedure inside to be extracted and run as code (**"Approximation capabilities of multilayer feedforward networks", Hornik, 1991**). A second, sharper objection follows from the first. A genuine algorithm, e.g. sorting, is correct across the whole of its valid domain, whereas a trained network is constrained to where it saw data. Off the training distribution, "all bets are off." On this view the very general application that defines an algorithm is exactly the property a learned approximation by a neural network cannot be trusted to have, as it is constrained by what it has seen during the training phase.

For the Levenshtein (edit) distance, the strong form of the first objection has already been settled, and instructively so. **Ohtomo et al. (2025)** construct a ReLU network that computes Levenshtein Distance *exactly*, by unrolling the dynamic-programming score-matrix recurrence into "matching" and "minimum" modules. The construction is a practical dead end: the network must be rebuilt for every input length (zero-padding changes the distance), its construction cost grows steeply with length (on the order of nineteen minutes at length 100), and the experiments are confined to a binary alphabet. In the end, the neural network at play is much more computationally expensive than the original algorithm itself. Most tellingly, the same network cannot learn by gradient descent, because the output stalls in local minima. So the exact algorithm can be built into a network but not trained into one.

What remains is therefore the question: Do we even need to model the algorithm exactly, or can we drop exactness for a “good enough” approximation? If exactness is dropped, a network does not need to be the algorithm itself. It can instead approximate the function the algorithm computes: the map from a pair of strings to their edit distance, in a form that is cheaper to reuse than repeatedly running the algorithm itself. This trade-off already motivates approximate retrieval methods for the vast sequence and structure databases that have recently become available **("Fast protein structure searching using structure graph embeddings")**.

Hence in the following, we do not claim to recover an exact replica of a runnable edit-distance procedure from a network but rather an approximation. 

Levenshtein Distance has played a central role in sequence alignment in particular and biological database similarity search in general **("Levenshtein Distance, Sequence Comparison and Biological Database Search", Berger, Waterman & Yu, 2021)**. The duality between minimum-edit distance and maximum alignment similarity **(Smith & Waterman, 1981)** lets us move freely between a distance and a normalised similarity, which can be used as a label during the training phase of a neural network.

Computing Levenshtein Distance exactly is expensive as we already know: the dynamic program is quadratic, and under the Strong Exponential Time Hypothesis no strongly sub-quadratic algorithm exists **(Backurs & Indyk, 2015)**. A metric-preserving embedding trades that per-comparison quadratic cost for linear-time encoding and an approximate nearest-neighbour index. This is the embedding principle introduced for edit distance by Dai et al. **(CNN-ED, 2020)** and developed for biological strings by Corso et al. **(NeuroSEED, 2021)**. The neural network discussed in this thesis shares its *deployment shape* — encode once, index, search — with structure-based retrieval systems such as Progres **(Greener & Jamali, 2025)**, although those occupy a different lane, since their ground truth is structural homology rather than a symbolic distance.

*Side note:*

*The strings used throughout are protein-derived (amino-acid sequences and their secondary-structure and 3Di re-encodings, drawn from CATH), but they enter this work only as a conveniently labelled symbolic corpus: **no biological claim is made**, and the ground truth is always the edit-distance value itself, never homology, structure, or function.*

The network built here is a single encoder that maps an input symbol string to a fixed-dimensional Euclidean embedding whose geometry is trained to preserve Levenshtein edit distance as a normalised Levenshtein score. The research question is then not whether the procedure can be extracted, but an empirical one: *how far off what the network has seen during training does such a learned approximation of Levenshtein edit distance/similarity actually reach?* How well does it transfer?

The approximation is realised as a learned metric embedding in the Siamese metric-learning lineage (**Hadsell, Chopra & LeCun, 2006**). A 141,184-parameter encoder maps each string to a 128-dimensional unit vector. It is trained with plain, unweighted mean-squared error on the continuous normalised Levenshtein score, using the parameter-free readout `1 − ‖e_a − e_b‖₂/2`: there is no prediction head, no class binning, and no loss weighting. Each string is encoded once, after which comparisons become vector operations and retrieval can use a nearest-neighbour index.

What separates a learned operation from memorised dataset statistics is whether it survives leaving the distribution and data it was trained on. The central experiment takes a single encoder, trained only on synthetically perturbed strings over the amino-acid alphabet, and runs it **frozen** in two off-distribution settings: on natural AA strings after synthetic-only training, and on symbolic alphabets it never saw — secondary structure (3 letters) and the 3Di structural alphabet (20 letters). Were the approximation just memorised amino-acid statistics, it should not transfer. If it captured part of the operation that edit distance performs, it should. The result is graded, and that grading is the empirical content: on 3Di and SS, overall Spearman correlation reaches 0.953 and 0.963, while full-pool MAP@10 reaches 0.515 and 0.405.[^rev-ss-2026-06-05] In the high-similarity band where retrieval lives, the same frozen encoder retains rank correlations of 0.874 and 0.862. ESM-2 reaches 0.709 and 0.148 there, and Dice reaches 0.282 and −0.240. The result is therefore not merely that some signal transfers, but that the methods carry different kinds of signal: SNNEED is the only evaluated method that retains high-band rank fidelity on both transfer feeds.

This motivates a two-fold claim, stated in the order of priority used throughout the thesis.

- **Primary — retrieval-grade approximation.** The encoder approximates Levenshtein well enough that embedding distance recovers high-similarity neighbours from full pools: MAP@10 is 0.515 over 347 3Di queries and 0.405 over 10,002 SS queries. The AA value, MAP@10 0.928, rests on only five positive pairs and ten directed queries and is therefore supporting evidence rather than the headline.[^rev-ss2-2026-06-05]
- **Secondary — off-distribution generalisation.** One frozen encoder transfers across symbolic representations, and from synthetic to natural strings, without retraining. The transfer is partial and regime-specific: position-pattern structure carries across alphabets, character-frequency-specific signal does not, and the advantage is concentrated in the high-similarity band.

This is not a claim that the learned encoder uniformly dominates simpler methods. Dice ranks AA similarity better overall (Spearman 0.474 versus 0.183) and ties or exceeds SNNEED on synthetic and AA retrieval (MAP@10 1.000 versus 0.972 and 0.928). Those feeds contain near-identical high-similarity pairs, where exact trigram overlap is near-optimal; SNNEED's empirical advantage is on the two structural-alphabet transfer feeds. Colab36 also shows that the bulk AA and 3Di score distributions sit within 0.001 of their own composition- and length-matched character-shuffled nulls, while SS sits below its null. The claim is therefore about recovering sparse, high-similarity neighbourhoods from chance-dominated pools, not about making all pairs uniformly similar or easy to order.

Scope is deliberately narrow. We approximate global, unit-cost Levenshtein (substitution-matrix costs and local alignment are out of scope, noted as future work). We claim approximation of the edit-distance function, not extraction of its procedure.

The scholarly comparison set is the family of edit-distance approximators: CNN-ED (**"Convolutional Embedding for Edit Distance", 2020**), NeuroSEED, and the training-free CGK embedding **(Chakraborty, Goldenberg & Koucký, 2016)**. Biological search tools such as BLAST and Foldseek remain outside that lane because they answer a different question against a different ground truth. ESM-2 is nevertheless included empirically: as a task-agnostic baseline on AA, and as a control on SS and 3Di for whether similarity signal survives when a large frozen encoder is fed the same re-encoded strings. The evaluated 35M-parameter ESM-2 has roughly 248 times as many parameters as SNNEED, but it is not treated as a peer edit-distance approximator.

The contributions are three. None is the embedding idea itself (that is CNN-ED's), and none is a biological result:

1. **Off-distribution generalisation as a distinct test.** A single encoder applied without retraining to unseen alphabets, and to natural strings after synthetic-only training: the algorithm-versus-memorisation probe that the per-dataset CNN-ED / NeuroSEED lineage, which trains and tests on one distribution, never poses.
2. **A mechanistic and regime-resolved account of what was learned.** *Position-pattern hashing* as the transfer mechanism (same characters in the same relative positions produce the same embedding, regardless of alphabet), an *alphabet-frequency-mismatch ceiling* that explains where and why the approximation leaks, and a band decomposition that locates the transferred signal in the high-similarity regime relevant to retrieval.
3. **Ablation-backed architectural diagnostics.** Adaptive pooling, rather than the classifier objective, is the load-bearing component; the final encoder therefore uses plain unweighted MSE with no head, bins, or loss weights. Colab36 further shows that pooling against the padded width preserves a useful length cue: true-length pooling fits the training task better but transfers worse.

The remainder proceeds as follows. Chapter 2 places the work among exact computation (Ohtomo), learned edit-distance embeddings (CNN-ED, NeuroSEED, CGK), and the broader neural-algorithms landscape. Chapter 3 details the encoder, the training-pair design, and the evaluation protocol. Chapter 4 reports the in-distribution approximation and the off-distribution transfer matrix. Chapter 5 discusses the metric-proxy bigger picture, the limitations, and the lane boundary.
